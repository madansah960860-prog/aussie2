eSIM, roaming or a local SIM: what Australians should do

There is no single right answer. There is a right answer for a five-day work trip to Singapore and a different one for three weeks across four countries. This guide gives you the decision, not a product recommendation.

By the Fieldnote editorial team Published 27/04/2026 Updated 21/07/2026 12 min read

Every Australian traveller has had the same conversation at the departure gate: does anyone actually know what happens to our phones when we land? The honest answer is that all three options work, they differ by a factor of ten in cost, and the cheapest option is not always the right one.

This guide explains how each works, what it typically costs, and how to pick. We do not name providers or quote current prices, because telecommunications pricing changes constantly and a figure published today will mislead someone reading in nine months. What we give you instead is the shape of the decision, which does not change.

The three options

In short:

  • International roaming. Your existing Australian number and plan, used overseas, usually via a daily pass. Nothing to set up.
  • Travel eSIM. A digital SIM profile you buy online before you leave and install on your phone. Data only in most cases, with a foreign number or none at all.
  • Local SIM. A physical or digital SIM bought in the destination country, giving you a local number and local rates.

They are not mutually exclusive. The most common good setup is your Australian SIM kept active for calls and text authentication codes, with data turned off, plus a travel eSIM carrying all the data.

International roaming

All three major Australian networks offer international roaming, generally as a daily pass: a fixed fee per twenty-four hour period that includes an allowance of data, calls and texts. Some postpaid plans include a number of roaming days at no extra charge, and some include unlimited roaming in a defined set of countries.

What it is genuinely good at. You keep your Australian number, so two-factor authentication codes from your bank arrive normally. Nothing needs to be installed. It works the moment you land. For a trip of three or four days, or for anyone who values not thinking about it, the total cost is modest and the convenience is real.

Where it falls down. Cost scales linearly with days. A daily pass that seems reasonable for a long weekend becomes the most expensive line item of a three-week trip. The included data allowance is often small, and exceeding it either throttles you or triggers an additional charge. Coverage depends on which local network your provider has an agreement with, which may not be the best one in the country.

Watch for: the definition of a day, which is usually your Australian billing day rather than a rolling twenty-four hours; whether the pass activates automatically on landing, potentially charging you for a two-hour transit stop; and whether cruise ships and in-flight wifi are excluded, because satellite roaming is charged at a completely different and much higher rate.

Travel eSIM

An eSIM is a SIM profile downloaded to the phone rather than inserted into it. Travel eSIM providers sell data packages by country or region: a set number of gigabytes valid for a set number of days.

What it is good at. Cost. For most destinations a travel eSIM costs a small fraction of daily roaming for the same amount of data. It is bought and installed before you leave, so you have working data the moment the plane lands. Regional packages covering, say, all of South-East Asia or all of Europe are excellent for multi-country trips because you do not change anything at each border.

Where it falls down. Most travel eSIMs are data only. You do not get a usable phone number, so anything that texts you a code needs another path. Calls have to go over data, which is fine for messaging apps and useless for calling a hotel landline. If something goes wrong, support is by email rather than a shop you can walk into.

Requirements. Your phone must support eSIM, and it must be carrier unlocked. Most phones released in the last several years support it, but plenty of Australians are still on older handsets, and some phones sold on contract are locked. Check both before you buy anything.

The setup most experienced travellers use

Keep the Australian SIM active with data roaming switched off, so calls and SMS still arrive at your normal number. Install a travel eSIM and set it as the data line. You get cheap data and your bank codes still work. Check your provider's charges for receiving calls and texts while roaming before you rely on this.

A local SIM on arrival

Buying a prepaid SIM in the destination country is the traditional approach and is still often the cheapest per gigabyte, particularly in South-East Asia where competition is fierce and prices are low.

What it is good at. Price, allowance and a local number. A local number matters more than people expect: ride-hailing apps, food delivery, restaurant bookings and domestic customer service all work better with one. In countries where you will be arranging drivers, tours or accommodation by phone, this is a practical advantage.

Where it falls down. You have to do it on arrival, tired, in an airport, often at a counter charging a premium over the price in town. Registration requirements are common: many countries require passport identification to activate a SIM, and in some the process takes half an hour. If you visit three countries you repeat this three times. And you lose access to your Australian number unless your phone holds two SIMs.

Practical note. Airport SIM counters are convenient and typically the most expensive place to buy. A convenience store or carrier shop in town is usually half the price for the same package. If you can get to your accommodation on airport wifi and a downloaded map, buying in town the next morning saves real money.

How to choose

Four common cases.

A short trip, under about five days

Roaming. The total cost of a handful of daily passes is small, and the time you would spend arranging anything else is worth more. This is particularly true for work trips where being reachable on your normal number matters.

A longer trip in one country

Travel eSIM for data, Australian SIM kept for calls and codes with data off. If you will be phoning local businesses often, or staying more than a month, buy a local SIM in town instead.

A multi-country trip

A regional travel eSIM. Changing SIMs at every border is the worst option here, and roaming across five countries for three weeks is the most expensive.

Travelling with family

One person on roaming or a local SIM, hotspotting to everyone else, is often the cheapest arrangement. Check that hotspotting is permitted on the plan: some roaming passes and eSIM packages restrict tethering.

What to do before you leave

  1. Check your phone is carrier unlocked. Ask your provider directly. A locked phone rules out both eSIM and a local SIM.
  2. Check eSIM support in your phone's settings. If there is an option to add a mobile plan or a digital SIM, you have it.
  3. Move two-factor authentication off SMS where you can. An authenticator app works without any mobile signal at all and is more secure. Do this weeks before, not the night before, because some banks impose a waiting period on security changes.
  4. Set data roaming off on your Australian line if you are using an eSIM for data. This is the single step that prevents a surprise bill.
  5. Install the eSIM at home, on wifi, before you fly. Most providers let you install the profile early and activate it on arrival. Doing this in an unfamiliar airport with no connection is the classic mistake.
  6. Download offline maps and a translation pack. Whatever connectivity you choose, offline maps mean a failure is inconvenient rather than serious.
  7. Save your accommodation address in the local script as a screenshot, so you can show a driver without data.
  8. Note your provider's overseas support number. The number on the back of your card is often a free-call number that does not work from abroad.

Six things that go wrong

  • The transit charge. Landing for a two-hour connection can trigger a full daily roaming pass in that country. Keep the phone in flight mode until you are at your destination.
  • Automatic app updates. A phone that downloads a large update over roaming data can consume an entire allowance in minutes. Restrict updates to wifi before you go.
  • The eSIM installed too late. Some providers require a data connection to install the profile. Install at home.
  • Losing the Australian number. Replacing your only SIM with a local one means bank codes go to a card sitting in your luggage. Use dual SIM, or move to an authenticator app first.
  • Prepaid expiry. If your Australian service is prepaid, check that the credit does not expire while you are away. A recharge from overseas can be awkward if the confirmation is sent by SMS to the service that has just lapsed.
  • Assuming coverage. Neither an eSIM nor a local SIM guarantees a signal. In Tasmanian national parks, the Otways or the Top End the constraint is towers, not plans. Download the map.

The short version

Under five days, roam. Longer than that, buy a travel eSIM before you leave and keep your Australian SIM in the phone with data switched off. In a country where you will be phoning people locally, buy a local SIM in town rather than at the airport. And whatever you choose, move your bank codes to an authenticator app before you go, because that single change removes the most common connectivity problem Australians have overseas.

Prices change

We deliberately do not publish current roaming, eSIM or local SIM prices, because telecommunications pricing changes frequently and an out-of-date figure is worse than none. Check current rates with your provider and with the eSIM seller before you buy. This guide is general information, not a recommendation of any product or provider.
