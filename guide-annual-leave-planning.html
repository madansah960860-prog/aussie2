Making annual leave stretch: a state-by-state calendar

Australians get four weeks of annual leave and live a long way from everywhere. The gap between those two facts is closed by public holidays, and by knowing that they are not the same in every state.

The standard Australian full-time entitlement is four weeks of paid annual leave a year, or twenty working days. Shift workers may get five weeks. That is the whole budget, and for anyone who wants to travel long-haul it is the binding constraint on the trip, more than money.

The good news is that twenty working days does not have to mean twenty days away. Used against the right weeks of the calendar, twenty days of leave can produce well over thirty days out of the country across a year. This guide explains how, and where the state differences matter.

The arithmetic of bridging

Every trip has two numbers: days away, and working days used. A Saturday-to-Sunday trip of nine days costs five days of leave. Add a public holiday on the Monday and it costs four. Add a second public holiday the following Friday, as happens at Easter, and a ten-day trip can cost three.

The ratio that matters is days away divided by days of leave. An ordinary week costs five leave days for nine days away, a ratio of 1.8. A well-bridged Easter can hit 3.0 or better. Over a year, choosing well is worth roughly two extra weeks of travel for the same entitlement.

This is the calculation the Fieldnote planner shows on every trip: total days away, working days used, and which public holidays you are relying on. It is a simple sum, and almost nobody does it before booking.

Holidays every state shares

Seven public holidays are observed in every Australian state and territory, although the precise substitution rules when they fall on a weekend vary:

  • New Year's Day, 1 January
  • Australia Day, 26 January
  • Good Friday and Easter Monday, moving dates in March or April
  • Anzac Day, 25 April
  • Christmas Day, 25 December
  • Boxing Day, 26 December, known as Proclamation Day in South Australia

Easter is the single most valuable block on the calendar because it delivers two public holidays four days apart, and in several states Easter Saturday and Easter Sunday are also gazetted. Anzac Day is the second most valuable, because in most years it sits close enough to Easter to allow one long trip covering both.

Holidays that differ by state

This is where planning gets state-specific, and where people who move interstate get caught out. The following are observed in some jurisdictions and not others. Exact dates are set each year by the relevant state or territory government, so check the official source for your year.

The four best bridging windows

1. Easter into Anzac Day

In years where Easter falls in mid-April, the gap between Easter Monday and 25 April is short enough that a single block of leave covers both. In the best alignment this is around sixteen days away for eight working days of leave, a ratio of 2.0, and it is enough for a genuine long-haul trip to Japan or South-East Asia. Book it early: everyone else can see the same calendar.

2. Christmas into New Year

The strongest ratio on the calendar. Christmas Day, Boxing Day and New Year's Day are all public holidays, and many workplaces shut down between them. Depending on the day of the week Christmas falls on, three days of leave can produce ten or eleven days away. The catch is that this is peak season everywhere in the Southern Hemisphere and peak fare season out of Australia, so the leave is cheap and the flights are not.

3. King's Birthday in June

An underrated window. For most states this is a June long weekend, four days of leave gives you nine days away, and it lands in the low season for both Europe-bound fares and Northern Hemisphere school holidays. It is also the dry season across South-East Asia and the Top End.

4. Your state's odd one out

Melbourne Cup Day, WA Day, Picnic Day, Recreation Day and Canberra Day are all Mondays or Tuesdays that most of the country works through. Domestic accommodation is cheaper on those weekends than on national long weekends, because demand is limited to one state.

School terms, if that applies to you

Australian government schools run four terms, and the dates are set independently by each state and territory. They are close but not identical, and the differences are the whole point.

If you travel with school-age children you are locked into these blocks and the aim shifts from avoiding them to booking within them early. If you do not, the aim is the opposite: the week immediately before or after a school holiday block is materially cheaper and quieter than the block itself, and the weather is effectively identical.

The one caveat is that these dates are approximate and change every year. Always confirm against your state education department's published calendar before you book.

Why the calendar moves prices

Airline and accommodation pricing is demand-based, and Australian demand is concentrated into a small number of predictable weeks. Three effects are worth understanding:

  • Domestic fares spike hardest around school holidays and national long weekends, because the market is entirely Australian. A Sydney to Hobart fare in the first week of October can be double the same seat in the third week.
  • International fares respond to both Australian holidays and Northern Hemisphere seasons. The December to January block is the worst of both, since it is our summer break and their Christmas.
  • Accommodation in small towns is the least elastic of all. Places such as Lorne, Coles Bay and Port Campbell have a fixed number of rooms, so a long weekend does not just raise the price, it removes availability entirely.

The practical implication is that shifting a trip by a single week, in either direction, is often worth several hundred dollars per person for no change in the experience.

Six tactics that work

  1. Book the bridge in the previous financial year. Easter and Christmas blocks are the first to be claimed in most workplaces. Put the request in as soon as the calendar is published.
  2. Travel out on a Tuesday and back on a Tuesday. Midweek departures are consistently cheaper than Friday or Sunday, both domestically and internationally.
  3. Use the shoulder of a school holiday, not the middle. Departing on the last weekend of a holiday block, when everyone else is coming home, is the cheapest flight of the fortnight.
  4. Check whether the destination has its own peak. Golden Week in Japan and Idul Fitri in Indonesia can matter more to your trip than any Australian holiday.
  5. Ask about leave at half pay. Many Australian employers, particularly in the public sector and education, allow annual leave to be taken at half pay, effectively doubling the time available. It is the single largest lever most people never pull.
  6. Consider purchased leave. Some enterprise agreements allow employees to buy additional weeks of leave through a salary deduction spread across the year. Worth asking about if a long trip is the goal.

A worked example

Take a Melbourne-based worker aiming for two weeks in Japan in autumn.

The obvious approach is to book fourteen days in October and use ten days of leave. The alternative is to build the trip around the Friday before the AFL Grand Final and the Melbourne Cup weekend, and to depart on the last Saturday of the Term 3 school holidays.

Structured that way, the trip runs Saturday to the second Sunday, sixteen days away. Two of the working days inside it are public holidays in Victoria, so the leave cost is eight days rather than ten. The outbound flight leaves on the day the school holiday traffic is returning, which is typically the cheapest departure of the month. The arrival lands in the best two weeks of the Japanese autumn.

Same trip, same destination, same weather. Two extra days away, two fewer days of leave, and a lower fare. The only difference is that the calendar was consulted before the booking rather than after it.

That is the entire argument of this guide. Build the dates first, then price the trip. The Fieldnote planner shows days away and working days used on every trip so the comparison is in front of you while you are still deciding.

Check the current dates

Public holiday and school term dates are set annually by each state and territory government and do change. The tables above describe the usual pattern, not a determination for your year. Confirm against your state government's official public holiday listing and your state education department's calendar before booking anything.
