Bali

Close, affordable and slower to move around than the map suggests. Choose two bases, not five, and the island rewards you.

01

Overview

Bali is a province of Indonesia, roughly 5,780 square kilometres, and the closest genuinely foreign destination for most Australians. From Perth it is a shorter flight than Sydney. That proximity is why it works for a week off rather than a career break, and why it has been the default overseas trip for Australian families and first-time travellers for two generations.

The island divides into a few practical zones. The south covers the airport, Kuta, Seminyak, Canggu and the Bukit Peninsula with Uluwatu: beaches, surf, restaurants, nightlife, traffic. Ubud, inland and about an hour and a half north, is cooler, greener, and built around rice terraces, art and temples. The east around Amed and Candidasa is quieter, drier and better for diving. The north around Lovina is the least visited and slowest to reach.

The mistake almost every first plan makes is underestimating travel time. Bali looks small. It is not fast. Kuta to Ubud is 38 kilometres and takes between 75 minutes and two and a half hours depending on time of day. Trying to base yourself in Seminyak and day-trip to Ubud, Uluwatu and the east coast means spending most of the holiday in a car. Pick two bases for a week, three for a fortnight.

Bali is also a living Hindu culture, not a resort with decoration. Temple ceremonies close roads without notice, offerings sit on footpaths and shopfronts every morning, and Nyepi, the Day of Silence, shuts the entire island including the airport for 24 hours. If your dates fall near Nyepi, that is a planning fact, not a curiosity.

02

Best time to visit

Bali sits eight degrees south of the equator, so it has a wet season and a dry season rather than four seasons. Temperature barely moves: roughly 27°C to 32°C in the day all year, cooler in Ubud and cooler again in the highlands.

Wet and dry season
PeriodMonthsConditionsAustralian context
Dry seasonApr to OctLower humidity, reliable sun, better visibility for divingCoincides with Australian winter, which is the main reason July and August are the peak months and the dearest.
ShoulderApr, May, Sep, OctDry season weather without peak pricingThe best value window for Australian travellers. May and September in particular.
Wet seasonNov to MarHeavy afternoon downpours, high humidity, green landscapeOverlaps the Australian summer school holidays. Cheaper accommodation, but January and February can deliver several wet days in a row.

What the wet season actually means

It does not rain all day. A typical wet season day is bright until early afternoon, then delivers an hour or two of very heavy rain, then clears. It is workable, and prices drop noticeably. The genuine downsides are reduced underwater visibility, some seaweed and debris on southern beaches, and the risk of a washed-out day when you had one thing planned.

Dates to check before you book

  • Nyepi, the Balinese Day of Silence, falls in March. For 24 hours nobody goes outside, no lights are used, and Ngurah Rai Airport closes entirely. Hotels operate but you cannot leave the property. Arriving or departing on that date is not possible.
  • Galungan and Kuningan, ten days apart, occur roughly every 210 days. Beautiful, and busy, with many businesses closed for family ceremonies.
  • Idul Fitri, the end of Ramadan, brings a large influx of domestic Indonesian visitors and lifts prices.
  • Australian school holidays in late June to mid-July and late September are the busiest weeks for family accommodation in Seminyak, Nusa Dua and Sanur.

Money notes for Australians

  • Currency is the Indonesian rupiah. Note the zeros: a 100,000 rupiah note is roughly $10. Check the amount twice at ATMs and when paying.
  • Use bank ATMs attached to a branch or inside a hotel or supermarket, not standalone machines on the street. Withdrawal fees are charged by both the local bank and your own.
  • Cards are widely accepted in the south and in Ubud, less so in the east and north. Carry cash for warungs, markets and temple donations.
  • Restaurants often add a service charge and government tax, commonly quoted as plus plus, which can add over 15 per cent to the menu price. It is legitimate; just read the small print at the bottom of the menu.
  • Tipping is not obligatory but is normal for drivers, guides and spa staff. Rounding up is enough.
  • A tourist levy applies to foreign visitors arriving in Bali. It is paid online or on arrival, and it is separate from the visa fee. Check the current amount before you fly.
04

Three-day sample plan

Two nights in Ubud, one on the Bukit Peninsula. Costs are per person, two people sharing, with a private driver rather than a scooter.

Day 01

Arrival and Ubud

Estimated day cost$142

  • 09:30 Arrive Ngurah Rai International Airport Immigration and visa on arrival can take an hour at peak times. Pre-book a transfer or use the official taxi counter rather than accepting an offer in the arrivals hall. Drive 1 h 30 min · 38 km to Ubud
  • 11:30 Check in and eat Most Ubud accommodation will hold bags before the standard check-in time. Nasi campur at a local warung runs about $5. Walk 20 min to the ridge trail
  • 15:00 Campuhan Ridge Walk A flat 2 kilometre ridge path with no shade. Late afternoon is far more comfortable than midday. Allow an hour and a half return. Walk 15 min into central Ubud
  • 18:00 Ubud Palace dance performance Legong or Barong, most evenings, tickets sold at the gate for around $10. About 90 minutes.
  • 20:00 Dinner on Jalan Dewi Sita Walkable, plenty of choice from $8 to $35 a head.

Includes the airport transfer, one mid-range Ubud room shared, two meals out and the dance ticket. Arrival day is deliberately light.

Day 02

Rice terraces, water temple and a waterfall

Estimated day cost$168

  • 07:30 Driver collects from the hotel A private driver for a full day costs roughly $60 to $75 for the car, split between passengers. Agree the itinerary and the price before you leave. Drive 35 min · 12 km to Tegallalang
  • 08:15 Tegallalang rice terraces Arrive early: by ten it is crowded and hot. Small donations are collected at several points along the path. Wear shoes with grip, the steps are steep and often wet. Drive 50 min · 25 km north
  • 10:30 Tirta Empul water temple An active purification temple. A sarong is required and can be hired at the entrance. If you enter the bathing pools, follow the order of the spouts and behave as a participant, not a spectator. Drive 1 h · 30 km west
  • 12:30 Lunch with a view over the Ayung Valley About $20 a head at a mid-range place. Drive 40 min · 20 km
  • 14:30 Tegenungan or Kanto Lampo waterfall Both involve a steep set of steps down and back. Entry is a few dollars. Swimming depends on recent rain. Drive 40 min back to Ubud
  • 17:30 Balinese massage An hour costs between $12 and $40 depending on where you go.

The driver is the largest line item and the best value purchase of the trip. Three temple or waterfall stops in a day is the practical maximum.

Day 03

Bukit Peninsula and Uluwatu

Estimated day cost$186

  • 09:00 Transfer south to the Bukit The longest drive of the trip. Leaving after the morning peak saves about 40 minutes. Drive 2 h · 55 km to Uluwatu
  • 11:30 Check in, then Padang Padang or Bingin beach Both involve stairs down a cliff. Bingin has the better warungs; Padang Padang has the easier access. Drive 15 min · 6 km
  • 15:00 Late lunch above the surf Around $25 a head at a cliff-side place. Drive 20 min · 8 km
  • 17:00 Uluwatu Temple and the Kecak fire dance Arrive by 17:00 for a seat at the 18:00 performance. A sarong is provided. Keep sunglasses, phones and loose bags secured: the resident macaques take things and trade them back. Drive 45 min · 22 km to the airport if flying out

Allow at least three hours between the end of the Kecak performance and an international departure. Traffic on the Bypass is unpredictable.

05

Getting around

Private driver

The standard and the sensible choice. A car with a driver for eight to ten hours costs roughly $60 to $80 for the vehicle, not per person. Agree the route, the hours and the price in writing before departure, and confirm whether fuel and parking are included. Most drivers are happy to wait while you walk.

Ride-hailing apps

Grab and Gojek work well in the south and in Ubud and are cheap. Some areas have local transport co-operative agreements that restrict app pick-ups; you may be asked to walk a short distance to meet the driver. Do not argue about it.

Scooters

Cheap, convenient, and the single largest source of serious injury for Australian visitors. If you ride one:

  • You must hold a valid motorcycle licence, and generally an International Driving Permit, to ride legally. Riding without one very likely voids your travel insurance for any accident.
  • Wear a proper helmet, fastened, and closed shoes. Most injuries are to feet, ankles and hands.
  • Road surfaces vary, rain makes them slick within seconds, and traffic behaves differently from Australia.
  • Check what your policy actually says about motorcycle cover before you leave home. Many Australian policies exclude it outright or require a licence for the engine size.

Fast boats to the Nusa islands and Lombok

Fast boats leave from Sanur and Padang Bai. Safety standards and sea conditions vary; crossings can be rough between December and February. Choose an operator with a visible safety record and life jackets for every passenger.

Where to stay by area

Ubud

Inland, greener and several degrees cooler. Best for temples, rice terraces, walking, yoga and food. Central Ubud is walkable; anything advertised as Ubud but 8 kilometres out is not, and you will need a driver for every meal.

Seminyak and Petitenget

Restaurants, beach clubs and shopping, with a wide beach and reliable sunsets. Busy and the most expensive part of the south for what you get.

Canggu

Surf, cafes and a large long-stay population. Traffic on the narrow lanes is now a serious constraint, so pick accommodation close to what you actually want to do.

Uluwatu and the Bukit Peninsula

Cliffs, the best surf and the quietest beaches, reached by stairs. Spread out, so a scooter or a driver is essential. Good for two or three nights at the end of a trip.

Sanur

Calm water, a flat beachfront path and a much older, quieter feel. The most practical base for families with small children and for anyone taking a boat to Nusa Penida or Lembongan.

Nusa Dua

Large resorts on a controlled beachfront. Predictable, sheltered and separate from village life, which is exactly what some travellers want and others do not.

Amed and the east

Black sand, dive sites including the Liberty wreck at Tulamben, and very little nightlife. Three hours from the airport, so worth it only for four nights or more.

07

Safety notes

These are the risks that actually generate consular cases and insurance claims for Australians in Bali. Check the current Smartraveller advice level for Indonesia before you book and again before you fly.

  • Scooter accidents. The most common cause of serious injury. Licence, helmet, closed shoes, and confirm your insurance covers it.
  • Drugs. Indonesia enforces severe penalties for drug offences, including long prison sentences and the death penalty for trafficking. There is no leniency for foreign visitors and no realistic consular remedy.
  • Methanol poisoning. Contaminated spirits, particularly local arak and cheap cocktails, have caused deaths. Buy from licensed venues, avoid free-pour spirits at unknown bars, and stick to beer or sealed bottles if unsure.
  • Water and food. Do not drink tap water. Use bottled or filtered water, including for brushing teeth in cheaper accommodation. Busy warungs with high turnover are generally safer than empty ones.
  • Dengue fever. Present year round, higher in the wet season. Use repellent, particularly around dawn and dusk. There is no vaccine widely available to travellers.
  • Rabies. Present in Bali. Avoid contact with dogs and monkeys. Any bite or scratch needs immediate medical attention, not a wait-and-see approach.
  • Ocean conditions. Strong rips on the west coast beaches. Swim between the flags where lifeguards are present, and take local warnings seriously.
  • Volcanic activity. Mount Agung has erupted in recent years and ash can close the airport at short notice. This is a real reason to hold travel insurance that covers natural disaster disruption.
  • Medical costs. International-standard clinics in Bali are expensive and often require payment or a guarantee before treatment. Medical evacuation to Australia can cost tens of thousands of dollars.

08

Good to know

  • Power. 230 V, plug types C and F, the two-round-pin European style. Your Australian Type I plug will not fit, so bring an adaptor. Most hotels will lend one but do not rely on it.
  • Connectivity. A local SIM or eSIM is inexpensive and far cheaper than Australian roaming. Coverage in the south and Ubud is good. See our eSIM and roaming guide.
  • Passport validity. Indonesia requires at least six months validity from your date of entry. Airlines check this at the departure gate in Australia and will refuse boarding.
  • Temple dress. A sarong and sash are required to enter temples, and are usually available at the entrance. Shoulders covered. Do not enter a temple while menstruating, which is a stated rule at many sites.
  • Photography. Ask before photographing people, ceremonies or offerings. Never stand on or in front of an offering placed on the ground.
  • Language. Indonesian is the national language and Balinese is widely spoken. English is common in tourist areas. A few words of Indonesian are noticed and appreciated.
  • Plastic and waste. Bali has banned single-use plastic bags and straws. Carry a refillable bottle; many cafes will refill it.
  • Departure. Ngurah Rai gets congested. Arrive three hours before an international flight, particularly during the July and August peak.
  • Vaccinations. Speak to a travel doctor or your GP at least six weeks before departure. Hepatitis A, typhoid and rabies pre-exposure are commonly discussed for Bali. We are not medical advisers.
