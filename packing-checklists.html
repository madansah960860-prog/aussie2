02

By climate

Add these to the core list. Expand the one that matches where you are going.

  • Waterproof and windproof shell with a hood. Not a fashion jacket
  • Insulating mid layer: fleece or a light down jacket that compresses
  • Thermal base layers, merino or synthetic, never cotton
  • Beanie and gloves. Most heat is lost through extremities
  • Walking shoes with grip, already broken in
  • Two pairs of wool socks per three days
  • Sunglasses. Alpine and snow glare is severe even at 8°C
  • Sunscreen. Cold does not mean low UV, particularly in Tasmania and New Zealand

The single most common mistake mainland Australians make in Tasmania is packing for the temperature and not for the wind. A 12°C day with a 40 km/h southerly feels like 4°C.

  • Light, loose, quick-drying clothing. Linen and technical fabrics over denim
  • One set of long sleeves and long trousers for temples, mosques and evening mosquitoes
  • Sarong or scarf for shoulder covering at religious sites
  • Repellent with an effective active ingredient, applied at dawn and dusk
  • Reef-safe sunscreen and a rash vest if snorkelling
  • Sandals plus one closed shoe. Closed shoes matter if you ride a scooter
  • Compact umbrella or a light rain shell for wet season downpours
  • Dry bag for boat transfers

Laundry is cheap and fast in most tropical destinations, so pack four days of clothing for a two-week trip rather than fourteen.

  • Broad-brimmed hat, not a cap. Sun protection for the neck and ears
  • Long-sleeved light shirts. Covering up beats sunscreen alone
  • Two litres of water capacity per person minimum
  • Warm layer for the evening. Desert nights fall below 5°C in winter
  • Fly net for the head between October and March in central Australia
  • Lip balm with sun protection, and moisturiser
  • Modest clothing that covers shoulders and knees for parts of the Middle East

  • Waterproof insulated jacket and pants, or plan to hire them at the field
  • Merino thermals, top and bottom, two sets
  • Waterproof boots with a real tread. Footpaths ice over
  • Ski socks, gloves, neck warmer, goggles
  • Traction cleats that slip over shoes, for icy streets in Japanese towns
  • Moisturiser and lip balm. Heated indoor air is extremely dry
  • Swimwear, for onsen towns where mixed outdoor baths require it

Hiring skis, boards and outerwear at the destination is usually cheaper than paying excess baggage from Australia, and always less to carry.

Travel adaptors by destination

Australia uses Type I at 230 V. This table lists what you need for the destinations covered on this site. Most modern chargers handle 100 to 240 V, but check anything with a heating element such as a hair dryer or a travel iron.

04

Carry-on rules worth knowing

General guidance based on the rules that apply to flights departing Australia. Always confirm with your airline and with the aviation security requirements in force at the time.

Liquids, aerosols and gels

On international flights, liquids, aerosols and gels in carry-on must be in containers of 100 millilitres or less, all fitting in one transparent resealable bag of about one litre. Larger containers go in checked luggage. Medication and baby formula are generally exempt but should be declared at the screening point.

Batteries and power banks

Lithium batteries and power banks must travel in carry-on, never in checked luggage, and airlines set limits on capacity. Spare batteries need their terminals protected. Do not pack a power bank in a bag you intend to check.

Coming home to Australia

Australian biosecurity rules are strict and enforced with fines. Declare all food, plant material, wooden items, seeds and animal products on your incoming passenger card. Declaring something is free; failing to declare it is not. If you are unsure, tick yes.

Duty free and currency

There are duty-free concession limits on alcohol, tobacco and general goods brought into Australia, and amounts of currency at or above a set threshold must be reported. Check the current limits with the Australian Border Force before you shop.

General information only

Aviation security, biosecurity and customs rules change and vary by airline and route. This page summarises common requirements and is not a determination for your flight. Confirm with your airline, the Australian Border Force and the Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry before you travel.
