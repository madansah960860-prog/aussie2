Travel insurance: general information for Australians

This page explains how travel insurance generally works, what the common sections of a policy do, and which exclusions most often cause a claim to be declined. It does not recommend a policy and it is not advice about your circumstances.

Important: this is general information only

Travel insurance is a regulated financial product in Australia. Fieldnote Travel Pty Ltd is not an insurer, not an insurance broker and does not hold an Australian Financial Services Licence. Nothing on this page is personal financial advice or a recommendation to acquire any particular product.

Before deciding on any policy you should read the Product Disclosure Statement and the Target Market Determination for that product, consider whether it is appropriate for your objectives, financial situation and needs, and if you are unsure, seek advice from a licensed financial adviser or an insurance broker who is authorised to provide personal advice.

Why Australians are told to hold it

The Australian Government's Smartraveller service puts it in plain terms: if you cannot afford travel insurance, you cannot afford to travel. The reason is specific rather than rhetorical. The Australian Government does not pay overseas medical bills for Australians, and it does not pay to bring an injured or ill Australian home. Those costs fall on the traveller or their family.

The figures involved are not small. A medical evacuation by air ambulance from South-East Asia to Australia is frequently quoted in the tens of thousands of dollars, and an evacuation from Europe or the Americas considerably more. A short hospital admission in a private international clinic overseas can run into five figures before any transport is arranged.

That is the exposure travel insurance exists to address. Everything else a policy covers, luggage, delays, cancellation, is secondary to the medical and evacuation section.

What a policy usually contains

Policies differ, and the only document that governs your cover is the one issued for your policy. That said, most retail travel insurance sold in Australia is organised into similar sections.

The excess applies per claim and sometimes per section. A policy with a low premium and a high excess may be the right trade-off or the wrong one depending on the kind of claim you are most likely to make, which is exactly the sort of judgement a licensed adviser is there to help with.

Medicare and reciprocal health care agreements

Medicare does not cover you outside Australia. Australia does have Reciprocal Health Care Agreements with a number of countries, and under those agreements Australian travellers may be able to access some subsidised medically necessary care in the public system of that country.

Three things about those agreements are commonly misunderstood:

  • They cover medically necessary treatment, not everything, and generally only in the public system.
  • They do not cover medical evacuation or repatriation to Australia, which is usually the largest cost.
  • The countries covered and the terms of each agreement change. Check the current list with Services Australia rather than relying on a list published elsewhere.

A reciprocal agreement is a useful supplement. It is not a substitute for insurance.

Exclusions that catch Australian travellers

Most declined claims are declined because of a general exclusion the traveller did not read. The ones that come up most often in reported disputes are these.

Motorcycles, scooters and mopeds

This is the single most common problem for Australians in Indonesia, Thailand and Vietnam. Policies vary, but many require that you hold a valid Australian motorcycle licence, that you hold any permit required locally, that the engine capacity is under a stated limit, and that you were wearing a helmet. Failing any one of those can void cover for the entire incident, including hospital and evacuation costs. Riding on an ordinary car licence is not sufficient in most policies.

Alcohol and drugs

Claims arising from an incident where you were under the influence are commonly excluded. The threshold is defined in the policy, and it is not always a blood alcohol number; some policies use a broader test.

Adventure and sports activities

Skiing and snowboarding, scuba diving beyond a certain depth or without certification, trekking above a stated altitude, bungee jumping, quad biking and many other activities are either excluded or require a paid add-on. If it is the reason for your trip, check it is covered before you buy.

Government travel advisories

Many policies limit or exclude cover for travel to a destination where the Australian Government has issued a Do Not Travel advisory, and some are affected at the Reconsider Your Need To Travel level. Advisory levels can change while you are in the country. Check Smartraveller before you buy and before you fly.

Unattended belongings

Luggage claims are frequently declined because the item was left unattended, in a vehicle, or not reported to police within a stated period. Most policies require a police report within 24 hours.

Known events

If an event was already public knowledge before you bought the policy, a named storm, an announced strike, a volcanic eruption in progress, cover for disruption arising from it is generally excluded. This is why buying insurance at the same time as the first non-refundable payment matters.

Pandemics and epidemics

Treatment of infectious disease outbreaks varies substantially between policies and has changed over recent years. Do not assume either that it is covered or that it is excluded. Read the specific wording.

Pre-existing medical conditions

Insurers define pre-existing condition in the policy document, and the definition is broader than most people expect. It can include a condition you have had symptoms of but not been diagnosed with, a condition under investigation, and a condition that is well controlled with medication.

The general process is that some conditions are automatically covered if they meet stated criteria, others require you to complete a medical assessment and may attract an additional premium, and some cannot be covered. Failing to disclose is the problem: an undisclosed condition can result in a declined claim even where the claim appears unrelated.

Under the Insurance Contracts Act 1984 you have a duty to take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation when answering an insurer's questions. Answer them fully and keep a record of what you disclosed.

Credit card travel insurance

Many Australian credit cards include complimentary travel insurance. It can be genuinely useful and it frequently does not apply, because activation conditions are not met.

Common conditions include spending a minimum amount of your prepaid travel costs on that card, holding a return ticket before departure, activating the cover in advance, and age limits that reduce or remove cover for older travellers. Coverage periods are often capped at a set number of days per trip, and pre-existing conditions are usually treated more restrictively than in a retail policy.

If you intend to rely on it, obtain the policy document from the underwriter, not the marketing page on the bank's website, and confirm in writing that your trip qualifies.

Reading the product disclosure statement

The PDS is the document that determines what you are covered for. It is long. These are the parts worth reading properly:

  1. The schedule of benefits. Limits per section, and the excess.
  2. General exclusions. Usually one section near the back that applies to every other section. This is where the motorcycle, alcohol and advisory exclusions live.
  3. Definitions. Words such as relative, pre-existing condition, unattended and public transport have specific defined meanings that differ from ordinary usage.
  4. Claims procedure. Time limits for notification, evidence required, and whether the insurer must authorise treatment in advance.
  5. Cooling-off period. Retail policies generally include a period during which you can cancel for a refund if you have not travelled or made a claim.

The Target Market Determination is a separate, shorter document describing the class of consumer the product was designed for. If you do not fit that description, that is a signal worth taking seriously.

Questions worth asking a licensed adviser

If you speak to a broker or licensed adviser, these questions tend to produce useful answers:

  • Given my destinations, activities and medical history, which parts of this cover would not respond?
  • Is the overseas medical limit capped, and does evacuation sit inside or outside that cap?
  • What does the policy require me to do before accepting treatment overseas?
  • How are my pre-existing conditions treated, and what did I disclose?
  • If I hire a scooter or a car, what exactly is covered and what is required of me?
  • What happens if the government advisory level for my destination changes after I depart?
  • What is the excess, and does it apply per claim or per section?
  • What is the cancellation limit and which reasons are covered?

If something goes wrong

If you have a dispute with an insurer, the first step is the insurer's internal dispute resolution process, which they are required to have. If you are not satisfied with the outcome, you can take the matter to the Australian Financial Complaints Authority, a free and independent external dispute resolution scheme. AFCA can consider complaints about insurers and can make determinations that are binding on the financial firm.

Keep every document: the PDS current at the time you purchased, your certificate of insurance, your disclosure answers, receipts, police reports and all correspondence.
