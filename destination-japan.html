Japan · Field guide 04

Japan

An overnight flight from the east coast, an hour of time difference, and a rail network that makes a two-week trip feel unhurried.

01

Overview

Japan is the rare long-haul destination that is easy on an Australian body clock. Tokyo is one hour behind Sydney in winter and two behind during daylight saving, so there is effectively no jet lag in either direction. The flight is overnight from Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane, which means you land in the morning and get most of a day back.

The country runs roughly 3,000 kilometres north to south across four main islands. Most first trips stay on Honshu and cover Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka, connected by the Tokaido Shinkansen. Tokyo to Kyoto takes about two hours and twenty minutes by fast train, which is the single fact that makes Japanese trip planning different from planning anywhere else: intercity travel is quick, frequent and reliable enough to schedule to the minute.

Australian travellers cluster into three groups. Snow travellers go to Hokkaido or Nagano between December and February, when our summer holidays line up with the Japanese ski season. Culture and food travellers go in spring or autumn. Family travellers go in the September and October school holidays, which land in an excellent shoulder period.

The main planning error is trying to see too much. Ten days covering Tokyo, Hakone, Kyoto, Nara, Hiroshima and Osaka is possible, and it means changing accommodation five times. Two or three bases with day trips out is a better trip.

02

Best time to visit

Japan is in the Northern Hemisphere, so its seasons are the reverse of ours. Our December is Japan's deep winter; our July is their hot, humid summer. This inversion is what makes Japan attractive to Australians looking for a real seasonal change.

Season by season, with Australian context
SeasonMonthsTokyo rangeNotes for Australians
WinterDec to Feb2°C to 12°CSnow season in Hokkaido and Nagano, which coincides with our summer holidays. Tokyo and Kyoto are cold, dry and clear. Book ski accommodation eight to twelve months ahead.
SpringMar to May8°C to 23°CCherry blossom moves north from late March to mid-April and lasts about a week in any one place. The most popular and most expensive period. Golden Week, late April into early May, is a domestic holiday block to avoid.
SummerJun to Aug22°C to 31°CThe rainy season runs mid-June to mid-July, then it becomes hot and very humid. Festival season, and typhoon risk rises from August. Cheapest flights from Australia.
AutumnSep to Nov13°C to 27°COur favourite window. Autumn colour peaks in Kyoto in the second half of November. Overlaps the Australian September and October school holidays, and prices are gentler than spring.

Dates to plan around

  • Golden Week, the last days of April into the first week of May. Domestic travel peaks, trains fill and hotel rates rise sharply.
  • Obon, mid-August. A major travel period with the same effect.
  • New Year, roughly 29 December to 3 January. Many businesses close, but temples are extraordinary.
  • Cherry blossom. Forecasts are published from January. Booking accommodation before the forecast is released is cheaper and riskier.

Where the money actually goes

  • Rail is the variable. A single Tokyo to Kyoto Shinkansen seat is a significant cost. Whether a Japan Rail Pass saves you money depends entirely on your route: it is worth it for a Tokyo to Kyoto to Hiroshima to Tokyo loop, and usually not worth it if you are staying in one region.
  • Food is cheap at the bottom and reasonable in the middle. A bowl of ramen or a convenience store meal costs a few dollars. A good izakaya dinner with drinks is comparable to Australia. Convenience store food in Japan is genuinely good, which is not true elsewhere.
  • Accommodation varies enormously. Business hotel rooms are small, spotless and cheap. A night in a ryokan with dinner and breakfast included is a splurge worth budgeting for once.
  • Luggage forwarding costs around $20 per bag between cities and is one of the best value services in the country.
  • Consumption tax is 10 per cent and is usually included in displayed prices. Tax-free shopping is available to tourists at participating stores on purchases above a threshold, with your passport.
04

Three-day sample plan

Three days in Tokyo, structured for someone landing off an overnight flight from Australia. Costs are per person, two sharing.

Day 01

Arrival, Asakusa and the east side

Estimated day cost$196

  • 06:30 Land at Haneda or Narita Haneda is 30 minutes from central Tokyo, Narita is 60 to 90. Buy an IC card at the station machine and load it before you go anywhere. Train 45 min to Asakusa
  • 09:00 Drop bags at the hotel Almost every Japanese hotel will store luggage before check-in without fuss. Walk 10 min
  • 10:00 Senso-ji temple and Nakamise-dori Tokyo's oldest temple. Busy from mid-morning; arriving at ten is the last quiet hour. Allow 90 minutes with the approach street. Walk 20 min across the Sumida River
  • 12:30 Lunch: soba or tempura in Asakusa Around $15 a head. Ticket machines at the door are normal, and staff will help. Train 20 min to Ueno
  • 14:30 Ueno Park and the Tokyo National Museum A low-effort afternoon for a day when you have been awake since a Sydney evening. Entry is modest and the collection is the best in the country. Train 15 min back to the hotel
  • 18:00 Early izakaya dinner, then sleep Resist the urge to push through to midnight. Eating at six and sleeping at nine resets you properly.

Arrival day is deliberately light and confined to one part of the city. Includes the airport train, an IC card top-up and two meals.

Day 02

Shibuya, Harajuku and Shinjuku

Estimated day cost$228

  • 08:00 Breakfast from a convenience store Onigiri, coffee and fruit for about $6. This is a normal Japanese breakfast, not a compromise. Train 25 min to Meiji-jingumae
  • 09:00 Meiji Jingu shrine A forested approach in the middle of the city. Quietest before ten. Allow an hour. Walk 10 min to Takeshita-dori
  • 10:30 Harajuku and Omotesando Takeshita Street for the noise, Omotesando for the architecture. Two very different streets three minutes apart. Walk 25 min south to Shibuya
  • 12:30 Shibuya Crossing and lunch The view from the Shibuya Sky observation deck is worth booking ahead for a timed slot. Train 10 min to Shinjuku
  • 15:30 Shinjuku Gyoen national garden A large formal garden, small entry fee, and the calmest place within a kilometre of Shinjuku Station. Walk 20 min
  • 18:30 Dinner in Omoide Yokocho or Golden Gai Tiny bars and grills, many with a cover charge. Check the sign before sitting down.

A walking day. Around 14 kilometres on foot in total, so wear shoes you have already broken in.

Day 03

Day trip to Hakone

Estimated day cost$254

  • 07:30 Romancecar from Shinjuku Reserved seats, about 85 minutes. A Hakone Free Pass covers the train plus the loop of local transport and is better value than paying separately. Train 1 h 25 min · 85 km to Hakone-Yumoto
  • 09:15 Hakone Tozan mountain railway to Gora A switchback railway climbing through the hills. Forty minutes and a genuine attraction in itself. Cable car and ropeway 35 min
  • 10:45 Owakudani volcanic valley Sulphur vents and, on a clear day, Mount Fuji. The ropeway closes at short notice when volcanic gas levels rise, so have a fallback. Ropeway 25 min down to Togendai
  • 12:30 Lake Ashi pirate ship crossing Touristy, included in the pass, and the best angle on Fuji if the weather cooperates. Boat 30 min to Hakone-machi
  • 14:00 Hakone Shrine torii on the water The queue for the photograph can run 40 minutes. The lakeside cedar avenue behind it is empty and better. Bus 45 min back to Hakone-Yumoto
  • 16:00 Onsen before the train home Several day-use bathhouses sit near the station. Tattoos are still refused at some, so check before you pay. Train 1 h 25 min back to Shinjuku

The most expensive of the three days because of the rail pass and the onsen entry. It is also the day people remember.

05

Getting around

IC cards

Buy a rechargeable IC card at any station on arrival. It works on trains, subways, buses and in convenience stores across most of the country. This single step removes most of the friction from Japanese travel. Australian phones can often add one to a digital wallet, which is easier again.

Shinkansen and the Japan Rail Pass

The Japan Rail Pass is sold only to foreign visitors and covers most JR services including most Shinkansen. Whether it saves money depends on your itinerary. As a rough test: a seven-day pass is generally worth it if you are doing Tokyo to Kyoto and back plus one more long leg. It is generally not worth it for a single return trip or for a trip that stays within Tokyo. Run the numbers on your specific route rather than assuming.

Reserved seats cost slightly more than unreserved and are worth it in peak periods. Large suitcases on the Tokaido line now require an oversized baggage reservation, made free of charge when you book the seat.

Luggage forwarding

Takkyubin services move suitcases between hotels overnight for around $20 a bag. Send the big case ahead to Kyoto, travel with a day pack, and the Shinkansen becomes far more pleasant. Hotel front desks arrange it.

City transport

  • Tokyo has two subway operators plus JR lines. Google Maps handles it correctly, including platform numbers and carriage positions.
  • Kyoto is a bus city more than a train city, and the buses get crowded. Cycling is genuinely good there.
  • Osaka is compact and easy on the loop line.
  • Taxis are clean, metered and expensive. Doors open automatically, so stand back.

Domestic flights

For Hokkaido or Kyushu, flying beats the train on time and often on price. Both major domestic carriers sell discounted fares to foreign visitors booked from outside Japan.

06

Where to stay by area

Tokyo: Shinjuku

The best-connected base in the city, with the Narita and Haneda airport links, the Romancecar to Hakone and a dozen train lines. Loud, bright and convenient.

Tokyo: Asakusa and Ueno

Cheaper, older and quieter, with good access to the east side and Narita. Better for a first visit that leans historical.

Tokyo: Shibuya

Central and social, with the best late-night food. Rooms cost more for the same size.

Kyoto: near Kyoto Station

Practical rather than pretty. Best if you are day-tripping to Nara, Osaka or Hiroshima.

Kyoto: Gion and Higashiyama

Atmospheric, walkable to the major temples, and priced accordingly. Worth it for two or three nights.

A ryokan, once

A traditional inn with tatami rooms, a communal bath and a multi-course dinner and breakfast included. Hakone, Kinosaki and Takayama are common places to do it. Book one night in the middle of the trip.

Snow: Niseko and Hakuba

Niseko in Hokkaido has the deepest reliable snow and the largest Australian presence. Hakuba in Nagano is closer to Tokyo and cheaper. Both need booking eight to twelve months ahead for January.

Safety notes

Japan has a very low rate of crime against travellers. The risks are natural, medical and procedural rather than criminal.

  • Earthquakes. Common and usually minor. Hotels provide instructions; the standard advice is to protect your head, stay away from windows and wait it out rather than running outside. Enable emergency alerts on your phone.
  • Typhoons. The season runs roughly August to October and can cancel trains and flights with a day's notice. This is a genuine reason to hold travel insurance covering disruption.
  • Summer heat. July and August humidity causes heat illness in visitors who are not used to it. Carry water and use the air-conditioned interiors.
  • Snow and cold. Hokkaido winters run well below zero. Australians routinely underestimate this. Proper thermals, not a hoodie.
  • Medical care is excellent and expensive for visitors, and many clinics expect payment at the time of treatment. Carry your insurer's emergency number.
  • Medication rules. Japan restricts some medications that are legal and common in Australia, including certain cold and flu preparations and stimulants prescribed for ADHD. Check before you pack, and carry a doctor's letter and the original packaging for prescriptions.
  • Cash. Still needed. Small restaurants, shrines and rural stations may not take cards. International cards work at post office and convenience store ATMs; many bank ATMs will not accept them.

08

Good to know

  • Power. 100 V, plug type A, the flat two-pin American style. Australian Type I plugs will not fit, so bring an adaptor. Most modern chargers handle 100 V without a converter, but check anything with a heating element such as a hair dryer.
  • Connectivity. An eSIM or a pocket wifi unit is much cheaper than Australian roaming and coverage is close to total. See our eSIM and roaming guide.
  • Tipping. Not practised and can cause confusion. Do not leave money on the table.
  • Rubbish bins. Almost nonexistent in public. Carry your rubbish until you find one, usually at a convenience store.
  • Shoes off. In homes, ryokan, some restaurants and temple interiors. Wear socks without holes and shoes you can slip off.
  • Onsen etiquette. Wash thoroughly before entering the bath, no swimwear, no towel in the water. Tattoos are refused at some establishments, though this is slowly relaxing.
  • Trains are quiet. Phone calls are not made on trains. Set your phone to silent, which is labelled manner mode.
  • Queueing is orderly and marked on platforms. Stand where the sign says.
  • Language. English signage is good on transport and thin elsewhere. A translation app with an offline Japanese pack handles menus well.
  • Passport. You are required to carry it. Hotels will photocopy it at check-in.
