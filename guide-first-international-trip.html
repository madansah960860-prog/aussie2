Planning a first international trip from Australia

There are about nine things that must happen before you fly, and three of them have lead times measured in weeks. Do them in the wrong order and you will pay more than you need to, or not go at all.

By the Fieldnote editorial team Published 19/05/2026 Updated 14/07/2026 11 min read

The first overseas trip is the one where you learn what you should have done six weeks earlier. This guide is the checklist we wish someone had given us: what to do, when to do it, and which steps have hard deadlines you cannot negotiate with.

It assumes you hold or are eligible for an Australian passport, and that you are going somewhere ordinary rather than to a country under a do-not-travel advisory. It is general information, not personal advice.

The order things must happen in

Most first-timers book the flight first because that is the exciting part. That is usually fine, with one exception: if you do not already hold a valid passport with enough validity left on it, book nothing until you have it in your hand.

The sequence that works:

  1. Check or apply for your passport. Six to twelve weeks before anything else.
  2. Check the visa and entry rules for your destination and every country you transit through.
  3. Pick your dates against your annual leave and any school holidays that affect you.
  4. Book flights.
  5. Buy travel insurance on the same day you pay a deposit for anything, not the week before you fly.
  6. See a doctor about vaccinations, at least six to eight weeks out.
  7. Book accommodation, and at least the first night's transfer.
  8. Sort money: a travel card or a low-fee debit card, plus a small amount of local cash.
  9. Sort phone and data.

Steps one, two, five and six are the ones with real lead times. Everything else can be compressed if you have to.

Your passport

Australian passports are issued by the Australian Passport Office, part of the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade. A standard adult passport is valid for ten years, a child passport for five. Applications are lodged at a participating Australia Post outlet or a passport office, and you need an interview appointment for a first application.

Two things catch people out.

Processing time. Standard processing is usually quoted in weeks, and it lengthens sharply before the December and Easter peaks. A priority processing service exists for an additional fee, but it is not instant and it is not guaranteed. Apply the moment you decide you are going.

The six-month rule. Most countries require your passport to be valid for at least six months beyond your intended date of entry, and some require six months beyond your date of departure. This is enforced by the airline at the check-in desk in Australia, not at the border. If your passport expires in five months and you are flying to Indonesia next week, you will not be allowed to board and you will not be refunded.

Also check that your passport is undamaged. A water-stained page or a torn cover can be treated as invalid, and again, that decision gets made at the gate.

Practical step

Photograph the identity page of your passport and email it to yourself. Keep a printed copy separate from the passport itself. If it is lost or stolen overseas, that copy makes replacement at an Australian embassy or consulate considerably faster.

Visas and entry requirements

An Australian passport gives visa-free or visa-on-arrival access to a large number of countries, which is why a lot of first-time travellers assume there is nothing to do. That assumption is where the trouble starts.

Check three things for every country involved in your trip, including places you only change planes in:

  • Whether a visa is required for tourism, and whether it must be obtained before departure or is issued on arrival. Some countries require an electronic travel authorisation that takes minutes; others require an application weeks in advance.
  • Passport validity and blank pages. Some countries require two consecutive blank pages.
  • Onward or return ticket requirements. Several countries require proof of onward travel at check-in. A one-way ticket to Bangkok with no onward booking is a common reason for being denied boarding in Australia.

Transit matters more than people expect. Changing planes in some countries requires a transit visa even if you never leave the airport, and if your itinerary involves collecting and re-checking bags, you have technically entered the country.

The authoritative sources are the destination government's own immigration website and the Australian Government's Smartraveller service. Do not rely on a travel blog, including this one, for a determination about your specific circumstances. Rules change without notice. See our Travel Advice Disclaimer.

Booking flights from Australia

Australia is a long way from most places, and that shapes every decision about flights.

Direct versus one stop. A direct flight costs more and is almost always worth it on a first trip, because a missed connection in an unfamiliar airport is a genuinely stressful introduction to international travel. If you do connect, leave at least two and a half hours for an international-to-international transfer, and more if you have to change terminals or clear immigration.

Departure city. Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane and Perth have the most direct international services. If you are in Adelaide, Canberra, Hobart or a regional centre, a domestic connection is usually involved. Book that domestic leg on the same ticket as the international one wherever possible: if it is a separate booking and the first flight is delayed, the airline has no obligation to you.

Timing. Overnight departures to Asia and Europe are common and are the reason arrival-day plans should be light. Arriving at seven in the morning after nine hours in economy is not the time to attempt a full sightseeing day. Our sample itineraries all build a deliberately quiet arrival day for exactly this reason.

Seasonality. Australian school holidays, particularly the December to January block, lift international fares substantially. So does the Northern Hemisphere summer. If your dates are flexible, February, March, May, September and early November are consistently the cheaper months out of Australia.

Travel insurance

Buy it on the day you make your first non-refundable payment, not the week before you fly. Cancellation cover only helps if the policy exists before the thing you need to cancel for happens.

Travel insurance is a regulated financial product in Australia, and we are not licensed to recommend a specific policy. What we can say is what generally matters, and what to read:

  • Overseas medical and evacuation. This is the part that matters. Medicare does not cover you overseas except in the handful of countries with a Reciprocal Health Care Agreement, and even then the cover is limited and does not include evacuation home.
  • Pre-existing conditions. Must generally be declared and assessed. An undeclared condition can void a claim entirely.
  • Activity exclusions. Riding a motorcycle or scooter, skiing, diving below certain depths and many adventure activities are commonly excluded or need an add-on. If you plan to hire a scooter in Bali, read this clause twice.
  • Alcohol and drugs. Claims arising from an incident where you were affected are commonly excluded.
  • Credit card insurance. Often exists, often has conditions you have not met, such as paying for the whole fare on that card. Read the actual policy document rather than the marketing page.

The document that governs your cover is the product disclosure statement. Read it, particularly the general exclusions. Our travel insurance guide explains the categories in more detail and directs you to licensed advisers. It is general information only and not personal financial advice.

Health and vaccinations

See a GP or a travel health clinic six to eight weeks before departure. That lead time is not padding: some vaccination courses require multiple doses spaced weeks apart, and some take time to become effective.

What gets discussed depends entirely on where you are going. Common topics for Australians heading to South-East Asia include hepatitis A, typhoid, and rabies pre-exposure. For parts of Africa and South America, yellow fever vaccination may be a condition of entry, and you will need the certificate. Malaria prophylaxis is a separate conversation with real trade-offs.

Two other things worth raising at the same appointment:

  • Your regular medication. Carry it in the original labelled packaging with a letter from your doctor listing the generic name and dosage. Some medications that are unremarkable in Australia are restricted or banned elsewhere; Japan and the United Arab Emirates are the two that most often catch Australians out.
  • A basic kit. Rehydration sachets, something for traveller's diarrhoea, painkillers, blister plasters and any personal items. Buying these at home is cheaper and you know what you are getting.

We are not medical advisers and nothing here substitutes for a consultation.

Money and cards

Three practical decisions.

How you will pay. A debit or credit card with no international transaction fee is the simplest answer, and several Australian banks and neobanks offer one. Multi-currency travel cards let you lock in a rate in advance, which is useful if you want certainty and less useful if the rate moves in your favour. Whatever you choose, take a second card from a different provider and keep it somewhere separate.

Cash. Some cash is still necessary in most of Asia, the Pacific and rural Europe. Take a small amount of the local currency for the first day, then use ATMs. Airport currency exchange counters give the worst rates you will see on the trip.

Dynamic currency conversion. When an overseas terminal offers to charge you in Australian dollars instead of the local currency, decline. The exchange rate applied is set by the merchant's provider and is almost always worse than your bank's.

Tell your bank you are travelling, or set the travel notice in the app. A card frozen for suspected fraud on your second day is an avoidable problem.

Phone and data

Australian international roaming day passes are convenient and expensive over a long trip. For most destinations, an eSIM bought before you leave, or a local SIM bought on arrival, costs a fraction as much. Our eSIM and roaming guide compares the three options properly, including the cases where roaming genuinely is the right answer.

Before you leave, whatever you choose:

  • Download offline maps for your destination.
  • Download a translation app with the offline language pack.
  • Save your accommodation address in the local language and script, not just in English.
  • Save the Australian embassy or consulate details for your destination.
  • Check that your phone is unlocked if you intend to use a local physical SIM.

The final week

A short list, and none of it takes long.

  • Check in online when it opens, usually 24 to 48 hours before departure.
  • Confirm the baggage allowance for every leg. Low-cost carriers within Asia and Europe are far stricter than the airline that took you there.
  • Check the current Smartraveller advisory again. Advisory levels change.
  • Leave a copy of your itinerary, passport and insurance details with someone at home.
  • Confirm the first night's accommodation and how you are getting there from the airport. Arriving at 23:00 with no plan is the single most common way a first trip starts badly.
  • Check the plug type for your destination and buy the adaptor here. Type C and F for Indonesia and most of Europe, Type A for Japan, Type G for the United Kingdom and Singapore.
  • Pack. Our packing checklists are free and sorted by climate.

The one thing worth over-preparing

Arrival. Everything else can be improvised. Landing in an unfamiliar country, tired, without local currency, without data and without a confirmed way to get to a bed is how people end up making expensive decisions at midnight. Book the first night, book or research the transfer, and know roughly what it should cost before you accept an offer at the terminal door.

Do that, and the rest of the trip is just a series of days you can plan one at a time.
