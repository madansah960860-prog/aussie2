Tasmania

Cool-climate walking, serious produce and driving distances short enough that a five-day trip does not feel rushed.

01

Overview

Tasmania is the smallest Australian state and the only island one, which changes the shape of a trip in two useful ways. First, distances are short: Hobart to Launceston is about two and a half hours, Hobart to Cradle Mountain about four and a half. Second, the weather behaves like nowhere else in the country, because the island sits in the path of the westerlies with nothing between it and South America.

Most first trips are built around Hobart in the south and Launceston in the north, with the east coast joining the two. That loop takes five to seven days at a civilised pace. Add Cradle Mountain and the north-west and you are at nine or ten. The mistake we see most often in submitted plans is a three-day trip that includes both Cradle Mountain and Freycinet: that is roughly eight hours of driving across two days, and both places deserve better.

The state also has a genuine food and drink case, not a marketing one. Cool-climate wine around the Tamar Valley and Coal River, oysters and abalone on Bruny Island, whisky distilleries around Hobart, and a farm gate culture that means half the good things you eat were made within an hour of where you are standing.

For an Australian traveller, Tasmania is the highest-value domestic trip on this site. Flights are short, there is no time difference, no visa, no currency conversion and no roaming charge. The trade-off is weather that will ignore your itinerary if you let it.

02

Best time to visit

Tasmania sits well south, so the seasonal swing is larger than most of the mainland. Read these as Southern Hemisphere seasons: December is high summer, July is deep winter.

Season by season
SeasonMonthsTypical Hobart rangeWhat it is good for
SummerDec to Feb12°C to 22°CLong daylight to about 21:00, alpine walking, festivals. Also the busiest and dearest period, overlapping national school holidays.
AutumnMar to May9°C to 19°CThe best all-round window. Stable weather in March, turning fagus in late April, and prices easing after Easter.
WinterJun to Aug5°C to 12°CDark Mofo in June, snow on the mountains, whisky and fireplaces. Some alpine roads close at short notice.
SpringSep to Nov7°C to 17°CWildflowers, lambs, changeable weather. Good value before the December surge.

Working around Australian school holidays

Tasmanian accommodation is finite, so demand spikes are sharp. The heaviest weeks are the national summer break from mid-December to late January, and the two weeks around Easter. If you are travelling from New South Wales, Victoria or Queensland, the April and September to October holiday blocks overlap enough to lift prices even though the exact dates differ by state. Travelling in the first two weeks of March, or the first two weeks of November, usually costs noticeably less for better weather than either shoulder implies.

What to expect from the weather

The local saying about four seasons in one day is close to accurate on the west coast and in the highlands. Even in February, a walk at Cradle Mountain can start at 18°C and finish at 6°C with horizontal rain. Pack a waterproof shell and a warm layer regardless of the month. This is the single most common thing mainland visitors get wrong.

Costs worth knowing about before you book

  • Parks pass. A holiday vehicle pass covering all Tasmanian national parks for up to eight weeks is far better value than paying per park if you plan to visit more than two.
  • Hire cars. Tasmania has a genuinely limited fleet. In January and at Easter, cars sell out and prices roughly double. Book at the same time as your flights, not after.
  • The Spirit of Tasmania. Taking your own car across from Geelong is worth it for trips of ten days or more, or for families with a lot of gear. For a five-day trip, flying and hiring is usually cheaper once you count the fare and two nights of travel time.
  • Fuel. Expect to pay more than the mainland average, and more again on the west coast. Fill up in larger towns.

Three-day sample plan

A southern loop based in Hobart. It assumes you land mid-morning and fly out on the evening of day three. Costs are per person, two people sharing.

Day 01

Hobart, kunanyi and Salamanca

Estimated day cost$186

  • 10:30 Land at Hobart, collect the hire car The airport is small and the queue is short, but book the car in advance regardless. Drive 20 min · 17 km to the city
  • 11:30 Salamanca Place and Battery Point Sandstone warehouses, then the Kelly Steps up into Battery Point. Allow 90 minutes on foot. Walk 10 min to lunch
  • 13:00 Lunch on the waterfront Around $32 a head. Fish punts at Constitution Dock are the cheaper option. Drive 30 min · 21 km to the summit road
  • 15:00 kunanyi / Mount Wellington summit 1,271 metres, and often ten degrees colder than the city with real wind. The road closes in snow, so check before you drive up. Drive 35 min · 24 km back to town
  • 18:30 Dinner in North Hobart Elizabeth Street has the density of good places. Book on a Friday or Saturday.

Assumes mid-range accommodation in central Hobart, hire car share, one cafe breakfast, one casual lunch, one restaurant dinner.

Day 02

Bruny Island

Estimated day cost$214

  • 07:45 Drive to Kettering ferry terminal Leave early in summer. The queue for the vehicle ferry can add 40 minutes. Drive 40 min · 35 km, then ferry 20 min
  • 09:15 The Neck lookout A 279-step boardwalk over the isthmus joining North and South Bruny. Twenty minutes, and the best orientation on the island. Drive 25 min · 22 km south
  • 10:30 Cape Bruny Lighthouse Second-oldest lighthouse in Australia, and the tour is worth the ticket. Exposed and cold in any season. Drive 30 min · 26 km north
  • 12:30 Lunch: cheese, oysters, berries The producers cluster within a few kilometres of each other around Great Bay. Allow $45 a head if you graze properly. Drive 20 min · 15 km to Adventure Bay
  • 14:30 Adventure Bay beach walk Flat, sheltered and good for a slow hour. Swimming is for the committed: the water sits around 15°C in February. Drive 25 min plus ferry 20 min, then 40 min to Hobart
  • 18:00 Back in Hobart Book the return ferry sailing ahead in peak season.

Includes return vehicle ferry, food from island producers and a lighthouse tour. A guided day tour from Hobart costs more but removes the ferry logistics.

Day 03

Port Arthur and the Tasman Peninsula

Estimated day cost$198

  • 08:00 Leave Hobart for the peninsula Check out first: you will not come back through the city before your flight. Drive 55 min · 62 km to Eaglehawk Neck
  • 09:00 Tessellated Pavement and the Blowhole Three short coastal stops within ten minutes of each other. Best at low tide. Drive 25 min · 22 km to Port Arthur
  • 10:30 Port Arthur Historic Site A UNESCO World Heritage convict site and a place that deserves unhurried time. Entry includes a walking tour and a harbour cruise. Allow three hours minimum. On site for lunch
  • 14:30 Remarkable Cave and Palmers Lookout Ten minutes from the historic site and usually empty. Good last look at the coast. Drive 1 h 30 min · 100 km to Hobart Airport
  • 17:30 Return the car, fly out Refuel at Sorell, not at the airport.

Port Arthur entry is the largest single cost of the day. Buying online in advance is usually cheaper than at the gate.

05

Getting around

Hire car, which is the honest answer

Tasmania is a driving destination. Public transport between towns exists but runs infrequently, and the places worth going are national parks and coastlines that buses do not serve. A small car is enough: the roads are sealed on every route in this guide, and a two-wheel drive handles everything short of a few gravel side roads in the north-west.

Drive on the left, as on the mainland. Speed limits are 110 km/h on the Midland Highway and 100 km/h or lower on most other rural roads. Roads are narrower and more winding than mainland highways, so allow more time than a mapping app suggests, particularly in the highlands.

Wildlife on the road

This matters more here than almost anywhere else in Australia. Tasmania has a high rate of vehicle strikes on wildlife, concentrated between dusk and dawn. The practical advice is simple: avoid driving between sunset and sunrise where you can, and if you must, reduce speed substantially. Most rental agreements exclude cover for animal strike damage.

Ferries and flights

  • Spirit of Tasmania runs vehicle ferries between Geelong and Devonport, with day and night sailings. The crossing takes around 11 hours.
  • Bruny Island ferry departs Kettering, takes 20 minutes, and takes vehicles. Book ahead in peak periods.
  • Domestic flights serve Hobart and Launceston from Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Adelaide and Perth, with Devonport and Burnie served from Melbourne.

Where to stay by area

Hobart: Battery Point and Sullivans Cove

Best for a first trip. You can walk to Salamanca, the waterfront and North Hobart, and you are 20 minutes from the airport. Battery Point is quieter and full of small cottages; Sullivans Cove is closer to restaurants and noisier on weekends.

Hobart: North Hobart

Better value than the waterfront, and the eating strip is on your doorstep. About 25 minutes' walk to Salamanca, or a short bus ride. Good choice if you have a car and do not want to pay city parking rates.

East coast: Coles Bay and Bicheno

Coles Bay puts you at the gate of Freycinet National Park, which matters if you want to walk to Wineglass Bay before the tour buses arrive. It is small and books out early for summer. Bicheno is 40 minutes north, has more accommodation and a blowhole worth ten minutes.

North: Launceston and the Tamar Valley

Launceston works as a base for Cataract Gorge, the Tamar wine route and day trips to the north-east. Staying in the valley itself, around Rosevears or Exeter, is quieter and puts you inside the cellar door cluster.

Cradle Mountain

Accommodation sits outside the park boundary near the visitor centre, and there is not much of it. Two nights lets you walk Dove Lake in whatever weather turns up, rather than gambling on a single morning. Book several months ahead for summer and school holidays.

West coast: Strahan

The base for Gordon River cruises and the West Coast Wilderness Railway. It is a long drive from anywhere, which is precisely why people either love it or resent the detour. Plan two nights or skip it.

Safety notes

Tasmania is a safe destination by any measure. The risks that actually cause trouble are environmental rather than criminal.

  • Alpine weather. Conditions on the Central Plateau, Cradle Mountain and kunanyi can shift from mild to dangerous within an hour, in any month. Carry a waterproof layer, a warm layer, water and food on any walk longer than an hour, and tell someone your plan.
  • Walk registration. Log your intentions in the registration books at the start of longer walks. They are checked.
  • Mobile coverage. Patchy to non-existent in national parks and along much of the west coast. Download offline maps before you leave a town. A personal locator beacon can be hired cheaply in Hobart and Launceston for serious walks.
  • Bushfire. Summer fire risk is real, particularly in the south and east. Check current warnings before travelling into remote areas and follow any road closure without negotiating with it.
  • Driving at dusk. As above: wildlife strike is the most common way visitors damage a hire car and hurt themselves.
  • Cold water. Sea temperatures sit between 12°C and 17°C. Rips on east coast beaches are unpatrolled outside a few main swimming areas.

Before you go

Emergency number in Australia is 000. Check current park alerts and fire warnings with the Tasmania Parks and Wildlife Service and the Tasmania Fire Service. Nothing on this page replaces official advice: see our Travel Advice Disclaimer.

08

Good to know

  • Quarantine on arrival. Tasmania restricts bringing in fresh fruit, vegetables, plants and honey, including from the mainland. Bins are provided at airports and on the ferry. Take it seriously, fines apply.
  • Power. Standard Australian Type I plug, 230 V. Nothing extra needed if you are coming from the mainland.
  • Mobile and data. Your normal Australian plan works. Coverage is the constraint, not cost. Telstra generally has the widest rural footprint.
  • Public holidays. Tasmania has state-specific holidays including Eight Hours Day in March and Recreation Day in the north in November. Regional show days close some businesses for a day.
  • Opening hours. Outside Hobart and Launceston, many kitchens stop serving at 20:00 and some towns have one restaurant. Book ahead or shop before you drive.
  • Cash. Cards are accepted almost everywhere, but a few farm gates and small markets are cash only. Carry $50 or so.
  • Wine and whisky. Cellar doors often charge a tasting fee that is waived on purchase. Nominate a driver; enforcement of drink driving limits is active and the limit is 0.05.
  • Aboriginal Tasmania. Palawa people have lived here for tens of thousands of years. Several tours and cultural sites are run by Aboriginal organisations and are worth choosing over the generic alternative.
